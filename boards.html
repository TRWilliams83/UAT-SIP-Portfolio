Perpetual Planter

The Perpetual Planter is an original reusable insert that converts a standard planter into a passive water reservoir. Fusion 360 was used to create and revise the internal geometry, define dimensions, and evaluate the part through structural simulation before physical production.

The design addresses overwatering, underwatering, and root rot while allowing the user to retain an existing planter. Its documented function, construction, revisions, dimensions, and alternative configurations support continued patent-development work.

  • Original functional product concept
  • CAD development and multiple revisions
  • Structural simulation and dimensional documentation
  • Patent proposal and product variations

Project C2: Capillary Canopy

Project C2 is a modular terrarium lid engineered to manage moisture and enclosure conditions through passive geometry. The system combines condensation routing, vent control, sealed fitment, and optional water-transfer features in a set of 3D-printed components.

The project progressed from an innovation claim through CAD revisions, physical prototypes, environmental testing, technical drawings, and a final SIP brief. This work documents the construction, operation, variations, and intended function of the invention.

  • Original innovation claim

  • Technical drawings and final SIP documentation

Screw School

Screw School demonstrates the complete transition from a digital model to a manufactured storage product. Fusion 360 was used to create the tray geometry, screw openings, stacking pegs, labels, and connection features. The parts were sliced, printed, assembled, and tested with actual screws.

Revisions corrected disconnected pegs, failed text, and slot geometry that did not guide screws properly. This required competent and safe use of CAD, slicing software, additive-manufacturing equipment, materials, and physical assembly techniques.

  • Fusion 360 design and revision
  • Bambu Studio print preparation
  • Multiple manufactured prototypes
  • Physical assembly and fit testing
View Screw Stack Project

Four-Gear Mechanism

The Four-Gear Mechanism is a working mechanical assembly made from separate 12-, 24-, 36-, and 48-tooth gears installed on a custom mounting plate. Center distances were calculated from the gear radii so the teeth would engage correctly.

The finished assembly was manufactured, assembled, and tested as a connected mechanism with an overall 1:4 relationship. The process required accurate CAD construction, mechanical spacing, component organization, joint setup, and motion testing.

  • Four functional gear components
  • Calculated mounting distances
  • Custom supporting base plate
  • Physical assembly and motion testing

Screw School

Screw School involved comparing PLA and PETG, evaluating 3D printing and CNC production, and repeatedly changing component geometry. The selected material needed enough flexibility to accept screws without cracking while remaining durable during repeated use.

Manufacturing tests showed that font size, nozzle capability, peg attachment, fillet depth, slot dimensions, and screw spacing directly affected the finished product. Prototype failures were used to guide material and manufacturing changes.

  • PLA and PETG comparison
  • 3D-printing and CNC-process evaluation

  • Manufacturing results used to guide changes

RBT Objective 2

Implement a simple microprocessor using digital logic design.

Related courses: RBT131, RBT173

8-Bit Processor Design and Validation

I designed and tested a complete 8-bit processor in Logisim Evolution by building the smaller digital-logic systems first and then integrating them into a working CPU. The finished processor includes an ALU, accumulator, registers, program counter, instruction ROM, data RAM, instruction decoder, control unit, status logic, buses, and an output register.

The processor executes a stored program one clock pulse at a time. The final demonstration loads 5, stores it in RAM, loads 3, adds the stored value, outputs 8, and halts. An additional clock pulse after HLT leaves the program counter and output unchanged, confirming that the halt logic actually holds processor state.

  • 8-bit ripple-carry adder built from full-adder stages
  • Two's-complement addition and subtraction
  • AND/OR operations, multiplexing, zero flag, and registers
  • Program counter, ROM, RAM, decoder, and control logic
  • Complete stored-program execution and halt verification

Status: Completed and validated in Logisim Evolution.

RBT Objective 3

Demonstrate embedded-system design skills, including microcontroller selection, schematic design, printed-circuit-board layout, design for electromagnetic compatibility, and design for manufacturing.

Related courses: RBT173, RBT211, RBT347

Gaia Controller PCB

The Gaia Controller PCB takes the Gaia embedded-system architecture from simulation into a manufacturable circuit-board design. The system was captured as a KiCad schematic and then transferred into a PCB layout with dedicated headers for the ESP32, OLED, SHT31 sensor, rain sensor, soil sensor, fan, pump, and external power connections.

The design includes separate 3.3 V logic and 5 V load interfaces, MOSFET switching stages for the fan and pump, flyback diodes across the inductive loads, gate resistors and pull-down resistors, and local decoupling capacitors. These choices support reliable embedded-system operation while also making the board easier to assemble, connect, mount, and troubleshoot.

  • ESP32-based microcontroller architecture
  • Complete KiCad schematic capture
  • PCB component placement and board layout
  • 100 nF and bulk decoupling on the controller power rails
  • MOSFET load switching with flyback protection
  • Accessible headers, labeled connections, and mounting holes

Status: Schematic and PCB layout completed; physical fabrication is a future phase.

Gaia Design Progression

The project documents a full embedded-system development path: functional behavior was first tested in simulation, the electrical architecture was then formalized in a schematic, and the schematic was converted into a physical PCB layout and 3D board model.

This progression demonstrates that the design was not limited to a breadboard or software-only prototype. The controller was developed with the physical requirements of connectors, power inputs, sensor headers, switched loads, board mounting, component spacing, and final assembly in mind.

  • Wokwi functional prototype

  • KiCad PCB layout
  • 3D board visualization
  • Design prepared for a later fabrication phase
